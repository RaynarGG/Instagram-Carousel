PSYCHOLOGY.

IT STARTED WITH A BASKET

OF MINIATURE CHOCOLATES

Spring 1998. One waitress with five years of experience, 80 dining parties, four conditions. A shuffled stack of index cards told her what to do at each table: no candy, one piece, two pieces, or one piece and then one more.

PSYCHOLOGY.

ONE PIECE BARELY MOVED IT.

TWO PIECES DID.

No candy: tips averaged 18.95% of the bill. One piece each: 19.59%, a nudge. Two pieces each: 21.62% — about 14% more than no candy at all.

PSYCHOLOGY.

SAME TWO PIECES.

A DIFFERENT WAY OF GIVING.

She offered one, then turned back at the table's edge and offered one more. Tips hit 22.99% — about 21% more than no candy. The amount of candy was identical. The gesture was not.

PSYCHOLOGY.

THE CHOCOLATE DIDN'T DO IT.

THE FAVOUR DID.

The authors point to reciprocity, not mood: an extra, personal gesture creates a pull to give something back — and the only thing on the table is the tip. The more generous she looked, the bigger the table's response.
